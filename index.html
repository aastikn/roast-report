A group-chat roast, with receipts.

This is an original comedic reading of the supplied WhatsApp export, not a personality diagnosis. Exaggerations are jokes; quoted lines and event descriptions are tied to export record IDs. Names follow the export's display labels. Contact cards, credentials, phone numbers, private links and irrelevant sensitive material are excluded.

The full export was parsed for counts; the narrative is an edited selection of retrieved exchanges, not a claim that every message received equal literary analysis. It spans 6 October 2024 to 28 October 2026: 121,168 records, including 120,884 human-authored messages, 135 Meta AI messages and 149 system events, across 13 human display labels. Missing media and deleted messages are not reconstructed.

1. The first word was "Oops!"

Tanush created the group as "ACM BOARD 25". Within minutes he had removed someone, re-added them under a different contact label, added Garv, and changed the title to MadarBoard. The first thing a human being actually typed into the new chat was Tanush himself saying "Oops!" (M000004). Not a mission statement. Not a welcome. An excellent opening word for what followed. The institutional body and the accidental body arrived in the same minute.

What followed was less a board and more a software project whose requirements meeting had become the product. For the next twenty-five months the same people kept inventing procedures to avoid the ordinary conversation that would have settled the matter in three messages. Polls became the constitution. Bill splits became scripture. The opening request for a shared photos drive already had the tone of a recurring support ticket: if enough people asked for infrastructure, perhaps the photographs would organise themselves.

The treats poll appeared within the opening evening. Kairav offered two options: "Meee" and "I am too kanjoos". Ten people claimed the first. Two admitted the second (M000038). It reads less like a menu than a public register of who is willing to fund lunch. Even opting out requires signing a declaration of kanjoosi. Kairav posted a message signed "From the desk of Kursi": 7484 for the main bill, 1400 for Amul, 8884 total, 685 per person if thirteen people were splitting (M000126). Aastik, watching the arithmetic, suggested they add four more rupees so the total would become 8888 (M000129). As a piece of comedy, this is not thrift. It is an aesthetic objection to the wrong repeating digits. The group had already begun treating money the way other committees treat minutes: something that must be published, corrected in public, and occasionally improved for symmetry.

Photos refused the same treatment. Aastik's first authored message in this export was "Aaj ki photos kiske paas hai" (M000010). Garv answered that Aradhya had most of them. Supratim asked for a photos drive, then specified "Aaj ki" and "Saari" in separate messages (M000042-M000046). At this point, the photographs had a contact person and the contact person immediately generated a request for infrastructure. A photo had not even reached a folder before it had become a systems-design problem. (M000010-M000046).

LinkedIn updates produced the same hesitation dressed as process. Sunny asked whether they could post that they had made the board. Eshita said wait a week. Garv said put it, with the force of someone who had already decided the official timeline was optional. Supratim offered to put it on his resume (M000020-M000028). In that exchange, enthusiasm and procedure were already pulling in different directions. The chapter existed. The public announcement of the chapter required a cooling-off period while Garv supplied the competing policy in considerably fewer words.

These were not side conversations. They were the operating system. A decision that could have been settled by three people talking became a poll so that the abstainers would have a record of their abstention. A bill that could have been split in a private message was posted with the assumption of thirteen participants so that the arithmetic would be inspectable. A set of photographs generated an immediate request for infrastructure. Even remembering the evening apparently required a storage architecture. The group had discovered that the meeting about the meeting was more reliable than the meeting.

Middle months did not break the pattern; they professionalised it. When recruitment forms appeared, Harshita spent messages describing how a badly designed question could create an infinity loop if someone selected a domain and then tried to decline further domains. Yash noted that one tool could not authenticate VIT users while another grouped questions more logically (M049998-M050008). The same people who had once polled whether names should appear on a T-shirt were now debugging conditional logic so that juniors would not be trapped in an endless form. The institutional impulse had simply changed objects. Instead of arguing about jackets they were arguing about whether Google Forms or another platform better prevented self-contradiction. The contradiction itself remained available.

The early merch debate treated every possible variable as open: T-shirt, hoodie, jacket, names or no names. Manan proposed "role based merch control"; Supratim answered the proposal with "Kursi madarchod" (M000156, M000158). Aastik proposed half-shirts, and the name poll tied five to five (M000201, M000214). A piece of fabric had acquired an access-control model before anyone could agree what to print on it. The clothes did eventually arrive. That did not mean the committee had run out of things to discuss.

None of this required malice. It required only the shared discovery that a procedure, once invented, could be reused. The treats poll established that contribution could be counted. The bill post established that the count would be public. The photos request established that memories could become infrastructure tickets. The form debate established that even the process of inviting new people needed its own process. By the time the group reached its middle months it no longer needed a fresh crisis to generate these rituals. The rituals generated the next ritual. A poll about dinner two years later still carried the same shape as the treats poll on the first night: someone proposes, someone questions the proposal, a vote makes the disagreement visible, and the numbers are settled afterward.

The first human word in the chat was "Oops!" The last visible coordination, years later, was still a booking, a late arrival, and a request for the split. Between those two points the group had built a small bureaucracy whose chief product was the record of its own small bureaucracy. The jokes were already present in the distance between the name on the group and the messages inside it. MadarBoard did not need an external satirist. It had renamed itself before it had finished adding the members.

3. A list, now with production support

A speaking queue is a list. Someone says a name, the next person talks, the list updates. Most groups manage this with a shared note or by simply remembering who raised their hand. MadarBoard, midway through a night of interviews, decided the list required a service.

Eshita had been keeping the order. Within a few messages the order acquired a name. People began issuing requests to it in the syntax of a database. Manan asked whether the thing was even real. Eshita answered that it did not exist (M064121-M064123). Tanush insisted it was real. Yash, already nostalgic, observed that Eshidis in its prime could guarantee one million concurrent message requests and that the current version was no longer the same (M064133-M064136). The service had acquired uptime, a golden age, and users who missed the golden age, all before anyone had confirmed it was running.

Yash later proposed renaming the improved version Better Better Eshidis (M067375). The double comparative did not resolve the underlying question of whether a simpler structure would have sufficed. It simply added versioning. Subsequent calls arrived as method invocations: Eshidis.curstatus(), Eshidis.asknext(), eshidis.add() (M068831, M068840, M070962). When a request failed, the diagnosis was technical. Garv asked about the handshake; Supratim answered with a CORS diagnosis (M062453-M062454). Garv asked whether a revised call would now succeed. The queue had developed client libraries, error codes, and the social expectation that one should check the changelog before speaking.

While interviews continued, Aastik treated the live night as a deployment window. He asked people to merge and reported switching the backing table's realtime setting on (M069664-M069678). Yasha observed that they were merging pull requests in the middle of interviews. The remark was accurate. A process designed to decide who spoke next had become a repository with branches, and the branches were being updated while the candidates were still answering questions. No one paused to observe that the original requirement - a sequence of names - had not changed.

The joke is the engineering overkill. A small coordination problem had acquired the social gravity of a platform migration. A list would have worked. A list would also have been invisible. By turning the list into a service with prime-time legends, method calls, and mid-interview merges, the group gave itself something to maintain, something to mourn when it felt slower, and something to page when the next person needed to be found. It grew the apparatus of a production system around a task that had never required one.

The warmest part is that the apparatus stayed funny to the people using it. They denied it existed, demanded its return, upgraded its name, and still managed to finish the night's conversations. The messages show people asking for turns, questioning the queue, and working on the code during the same interview session. What remained was the shared joke of having built versioned infrastructure for the oldest coordination problem in any meeting: remembering whose turn it is. They could have used a list. They built a service instead, then kept talking to it, and to each other, through the interview session.

5. The part that is not a joke

They kept one chat long enough for the same recurring objects to become recognisable. The merch that required polls, hostage language, and sample rejections eventually reached people in conditions that made the earlier design arguments feel ornamental. The speaking order that Eshita maintained and then denied was versioned, grieved, and paged in database syntax by everyone else. South Zone kept arriving as an alert whose definition had to be re-derived on the spot. Paneer puffs outlasted vendor negotiations and became both side-bet currency and registration-fee anchor. These exchanges rarely have the tidy rhythm of a project handover. They simply generated the next message that proved the previous procedure had been one step short.

The affection sits in the fact that the messages continued after the procedures failed. People still answered when someone asked who was free to collect merch, who owed two puffs, or whether the queue still existed. They split published numbers, coordinated another dinner (M121090, M121130, M121154-M121156), and kept the nickname and the formal title in the same flat without forcing a resolution. The archive does not show a perfectly run chapter. It shows a group that learned the specific shape of one another's delays, design reviews, and small concrete exchanges, and kept typing anyway. That continuity is what the record actually sustains.
