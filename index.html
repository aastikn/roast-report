MadarBoard: Thirteen People, One Pending Decision

October 2024–October 2026. Quotes are verbatim; unquoted chat scenes are paraphrased.

The first message Tanush Golwala sent was “Oops!” (06/10/24). He had added the wrong Shambhavi. Four minutes after creating ACM BOARD 25, he renamed it MadarBoard.

An unusually efficient launch: admission error, personnel correction, reputational collapse. Most institutions take decades to become a parody of themselves. You managed it before dinner.

Two years later, 121,019 messages survive. Somewhere in that archaeological deposit is a decision. Nobody has found it because Garv is uploading another attachment, Aastik has a better idea, and Eshita has rejected the request.

1. An institution with the decision-making capacity of a revolving door

On your first evening, Sunny Gogoi asked “LinkedIn mein update daal sakte hai ki nahi?” (06/10/24). The board was four hours old. The organisation had barely developed object permanence and already wanted professional recognition.

This set the constitutional order: first acquire the title, then discuss the clothes, then establish whether anybody actually knows what the title involves. A student chapter administered like a royal wedding where all thirteen guests believe they are the groom.

Manan Shah’s inaugural address on 07/10/24 was a long, earnest promise of service, transparency and improvement. Thirty minutes later, Eshita was asking for the bill split. There it is: the entire history of government. One man announces a new era; somebody else wants the ice-cream money.

Your finest institutional self-assessment arrived from Manan on 08/06/25: “our board is only not on board rn” (08/06/25). Beautiful. An organisation whose own governing body requires onboarding. Thirteen people at the wheel, all insisting the others are passengers, nobody having checked whether this is a car.

Even agreement became industrial waste. Across the export, Yash sent exactly ok 100 times; Aastik, 91; Garv, 78, ignoring case. That is 269 acknowledgements from three people. You could build a modest institution out of the things those acknowledgements were meant to acknowledge. Instead you built the world’s most elaborate delivery-receipt simulator.

The group is called a board because a committee would imply movement. A board just lies there while someone paints another title on it.

2. Merch: the only fabric with a governance problem

On 07/10/24, Manan proposed “role based merch control” (07/10/24). You had been a board for less than a day and had already invented permissions for cotton.

Normal people buy a T-shirt. You wanted the sleeve to authenticate your organisational seniority. Somewhere a plain black tee was waiting to become a garment, unaware it first needed to pass an interview and disclose its preferred domain.

The debate covered shirts, T-shirts, jackets, hoodies, names, positions, usernames, character limits and whether names would remain visible beneath the hood. You were designing clothes for Vellore with the strategic urgency of an expedition to a place where everyone recognises your GitHub handle.

Supratim’s contribution was “Or we make digital merch” (07/10/24). Finally, a product that could be delivered with the same physical substance as the consensus.

By 28/12/24, Tanush’s proposed workflow already included ordering, supplier errors and redelivery. That is not a contingency plan. That is booking the return journey from disappointment before buying the ticket out.

Manan nevertheless promised “aaj raat tak ho jaayega, and message shall be sent (hopefully)” (28/12/24). shall in the front, hopefully in the back: the official ACM fit.

By 28/11/25, Yasha needed reminding to bring the merch box; the next day, Manan asked “Acm merch kab aaya” (29/11/25). The man who wanted clothing permissions had missed the clothing.

Your real merchandise was anticipation. The hoodies were just packaging.

3. Aastik Narang: a suggestion box that has learned to follow you home

Aastik, you contributed 15,472 messages to this archive and then commissioned a roast of it. Even your self-awareness requires thirteen people to attend a launch.

On 07/10/24, after the bill total became 8,884, you suggested “4 rupay ka kuch aur lelete 8888 hojaata” (07/10/24). This is your mind in miniature: presented with a finished transaction, you discover an opportunity to make everybody do one more thing for absolutely no benefit.

Not even the bill is allowed to leave without aesthetic feedback.

That same day you declared “I don’t want to promote individualism” (07/10/24). This was during a merch discussion you had interrupted with an announcement of a unique idea and a proposal for half shirts. You oppose individualism in precisely the way a man holding the only microphone opposes noise pollution.

You had correctly identified that Vellore is hot. Your solution was a T-shirt plus an overshirt. Aastik can turn even fewer clothes into more clothes. Somewhere an air conditioner heard that proposal and applied for another room.

Then came turf. On 28/11/24, you suggested “We’ll take bedsheet aur something, turf pr bicha kr picnic 😂😂” (28/11/24). You wanted to hire a sports surface and domesticate it. Pay by the hour to sit on a bedsheet: the first picnic with a utilisation problem.

Exactly a year later, on 28/11/25, you wanted boxing instead. Supratim asked where. Your answer was “Ptani” (28/11/25). Your follow-up was “But fun hoga” (28/11/25).

There is the complete Aastik business plan. Slide one: activity. Slide two: no location. Slide three: vibes reiterated at a slightly higher valuation.

You don’t make plans. You introduce side quests into plans that were almost finished. Thirteen people are about to book a turf, and suddenly everyone has to investigate the local boxing economy because you experienced a thought.

But your masterpiece was the favourite-board saga on 14/06/25. You announced “mera bas chale to board rank karvau isse” (14/06/25). Eight minutes later, after the reactions and scoreboard chatter turned towards you, you wrote “this feels thoda sa unfair tbh ;-;” (14/06/25).

Eight minutes from commissioner of the league to complainant at the integrity tribunal. Even a printer takes longer to discover that the paper it requested is somehow the wrong paper.

Then you supplied the defence: “isko 4 baar pehle bully kra tha merko fav board bolne ko” (14/06/25). Your defence invoked four earlier rounds of pressure to make the candidate name you favourite. Apparently this was meant to establish the reliability of the polling. Aastik, that is a customer testimonial with a supervisor standing behind it.

You wanted a popularity contest with customer support. Four rounds of pressure! Most people would worry that this compromised the result. You were upset that the warranty had expired.

The truly punishing detail is your 153 standalone ++ messages. A man with boundless proposals, outsourcing 153 separate acts of agreement to punctuation. You can invent boxing without a venue but cannot invent a second sentence when somebody else has the floor. Even your endorsement wants the visibility of a new notification.

And when your own farewell approached, you wrote “Mere bina farewell to mt kro yar 😔😔” (06/11/25). Of course. Aastik cannot permit even an event about leaving to proceed in his absence.

You are the optional update that restarts the whole laptop. You are the person a poll is invented to contain, who then asks whether the poll should have another option. You are why a finished bill needs four more rupees.

And now you want a better roast. Fair enough. You have finally requested something for which making it about you is the actual job.

4. Garv Jain: the away message that sent another 126 messages

Garv’s 21,970 messages make him the group’s largest contributor. But volume is the least interesting part. The achievement is producing that much output while still occasionally asking what is going on.

On 08/01/25, Garv discovered sponsorship information and explained “Nahi dekha saara 😭” (08/01/25). Yash had just reminded him that messages arrive in the group. Garv inhabits WhatsApp the way a supermarket owns groceries: everything passes through him, very little is absorbed.

Then, on 15/08/25 at 12:46, he announced “If y’all need anything from me please call. I’m thoda afk till 4 pm” (15/08/25).

What followed was a run of 127 consecutive Garv entries, including that announcement: 125 omitted-media entries and one blank entry, ending at 13:02. No intervening speaker.

He went AFK the way a burst pipe goes off duty.

This is not exaggeration about someone texting a lot. He announced his absence, then occupied the entire channel for sixteen minutes. Other people’s out-of-office replies redirect you to a colleague. Garv’s redirects you to 125 attachments.

His 317 standalone yes messages and 228 real messages are less vocabulary than a pair of turnstiles. Everything enters; everything gets stamped. Then in October 2026 he asks what the dinner plan is, despite the discussion already happening above him.

Garv is a CCTV camera with push notifications: extraordinary coverage, no evidence that anyone is watching the footage.

The most frightening thing about his AFK declaration is that he apparently considers this reduced service.

5. Yash Raj Singh: the Reserve Bank of Paneer

Yash approaches small amounts of money with the gravitas of a man whose signature appears on them.

On 14/06/25, a bet about a candidate’s favourite board member became a two-paneer-puff position. By 23:26, Yash had announced “Paneer Puff ke saath no khilwad” (14/06/25). One minute later: “2 puffs i’LL LOSE” (14/06/25).

You could hear the markets opening. Somewhere a bakery was being designated systemically important.

This was no isolated snack opinion. On 02/08/25, while discussing event registration fees, Aastik valued fifty rupees at one chocolava. Yash countered “50 is 2 Paneer Puffs 😭” (02/08/25). Two economists, rival baskets of goods, absolutely nobody asking for snack-indexed monetary policy.

Yash’s gift is making responsibility look like a hostage situation he volunteered for. In October 2026 he initiated the Bengaluru meet, chased the time, asked for a table booking, tried to book it himself, and helped Eshita find the booking details. He had become the restaurant’s unofficial front desk before arriving at the restaurant.

Then, four days after the dinner, came “Split?” (15/10/26).

One word. Years of leadership distilled into a small unpaid invoice.

There will be no dramatic farewell for Yash. He will remain in the chat until the last ₹12 is accounted for, then leave a spreadsheet with conditional formatting that makes future generations feel watched.

6. Yasha Pacholee: institutional memory, except for the institution’s box

Yasha is the person who can turn an awkward moment into four lines of cross-examination before the original sender has finished pretending it was deliberate.

On the group’s first night, she inspected Garv’s last-minute upload with a sequence of questions, including “Is it the fact that the pdf is a copy” (06/10/24). The board hadn’t settled in; Yasha had already opened discovery.

She later described a group message as “more of a procrastination than an acknowledgement” (14/11/24). That is a beautifully precise indictment from somebody voluntarily remaining in a chat where procrastination has an acknowledgement layer.

Her research instincts were alive during the June interviews too. On 19/06/25 she complained “bhai me question pe question banaye ja rahi thi unneded because no one was adding to eshidis” (19/06/25). The queue was not working, so she manufactured additional demand. The escalator is broken; better keep sending people upstairs.

And then, on 28/11/25, concerning the merch box: “I have forgotten at least 12 times by now” (28/11/25).

Twelve times. She remembered the failure count. She had retained the analytics while losing the object.

Yasha’s memory is an exquisitely indexed archive of everybody else’s nonsense with one empty shelf labelled thing I need to bring. She can reconstruct the motive, means and opportunity behind a PDF filename. Ask her to move a box and suddenly the evidence has disappeared.

7. Supratim Ghose: the load test has become curious about the server

Supratim treats attention as a resource that must occasionally be reclaimed from others.

On 07/06/25, he complained “Itna aacha joke ignore kaise kr diya logon ne” (07/06/25). A joke with an escalation procedure. He wasn’t merely expecting laughter; he wanted to know which department had failed to process it.

During the favourite-board contest, he requested “fav board pucho :)” (14/06/25), then later instructed “manan update score” (14/06/25). Nothing says relaxed mentor like a live popularity ledger. These were interviews for new leadership; Suppu had installed a personal man-of-the-match ceremony inside them.

His greatest moment came at 03:35 on 02/07/25, deep inside a conversation ricocheting between music, Marvel, anime and childhood television: “Bc itni tez state management kaise krta hai WhatsApp” (02/07/25).

There is something majestic about being part of the traffic jam and suddenly becoming interested in civil engineering.

Suppu looked at the noise he was helping generate and saw a technical achievement. A kitchen blender wondering how the chef tolerates such vibration. His next message was an observation about the quantity of messages. He benchmarked the problem by adding another sample.

Then, after 25/06/26, he went 108 days between appearances. The load test finally paused. WhatsApp presumably lowered its cooling bill.

8. Manan Shah: a chair with an attached lectern

Manan began with a carefully composed inauguration. By lunchtime on the same day he was specifying which junior cohort should receive which style of T-shirt.

That is the danger of a chairperson with ideas. You ask him to lead the chapter; he notices the chapter has no dress-based permission hierarchy.

On 16/05/25, during a vocabulary spiral, Srija eventually told him “Okay Manan you can close the dictionary” (16/05/25). He continued. Naturally. A man who opens his tenure with a keynote does not let the public end his thesaurus demonstration.

Manan can make a small conversational gap feel like the allotted space for his remarks. Even nonsense arrives wearing the conviction that it ought to be in the minutes.

Then Eshidis happened. On 13/06/25, after finding that the speaking queue did not seem to recognise his authority, he demanded “BKLODO IS ESHIDIS A MYTH” (13/06/25). A minute later: “toh main kab bolu” (13/06/25).

The chairperson, asking a fictional database for permission to speak. That is not merely lost authority. That is having your office furniture repossessed by a running joke.

Manan wants to be the man who anticipates every possible failure. The group keeps supplying failures he could not have conceived, such as being outranked by Eshita’s queue bit. He wrote the inaugural speech for a ship and discovered he was chairing the lifeboat entertainment committee.

9. Eshita Chokhani: customer service has read your ticket and dislikes you

Eshita entered the chat asking for the bill and the farewell date. Within hours she was telling Sunny to wait before announcing himself on LinkedIn. Somebody had to stop the board from changing its bio before learning where the exits were.

But her masterpiece was Eshidis, the mock speaking-queue service built around her during interviews. On 12/06/25 she returned “no queue found” (12/06/25). When they kept trying, the response became “person calling the object is not good, request rejected” (12/06/25).

Most software validates the input. Eshita validates your worthiness to have supplied it.

This is what happens when a repeatedly summoned person in a group discovers that a technical error message can express contempt without starting a meeting. She had turned customer support into a velvet-rope nightclub, with Garv standing outside trying different pronunciations of the venue’s name.

On 13/06/25, while others insisted the service was real, Eshita said “it does not exist bhai” (13/06/25). Later: “all requests rejected” (13/06/25).

A non-existent service rejecting real customers. Finally, a startup business model with no overheads.

And on 15/10/26, when Yash asked about the dinner split, she replied “Oh dalti hu” (15/10/26). The person who began this institution by chasing its bill had become the person being chased for the bill. Eshidis had acquired technical debt.

10. Tanush Golwala: the building inspector who installed the slide

Tanush created the group, corrected an admission mistake and gave it the name MadarBoard. The next day he warned “Bhai we are still a part of ACM and a formal chapter” (07/10/24).

This is the man who spray-painted the lift reminding you that the building has standards.

His formal-chapter objection concerned Aastik’s clothing proposal. Apparently the institution could be named MadarBoard, but a half shirt would compromise the brand. The man has drawn an ethical line, and that line is a sleeve.

Tanush was also the one supplying the merch workflow that explicitly budgeted for errors and redelivery. That is his particular managerial flavour: strict about the rules, impressively realistic about the disaster, unable to resist participating in the disaster’s group chat.

He posted on 123 consecutive dates ending 26/09/25. Four months of uninterrupted attendance in the institution he occasionally remembered to call formal. You weren’t just maintaining standards, Tanush. You were clocking in at the scene.

And at the October 2026 dinner, when Garv said he would arrive in ten minutes, Tanush replied “Arey same… Mai bhi 10 min me nikalne waala hu” (11/10/26).

Same: one man arriving, the other preparing to leave. Tanush has achieved compatibility by ignoring the endpoint. Even his lateness passes code review if you only inspect the variable names.

11. Harshita Kaskyap: one more blog and then we can all go home

Harshita is the board’s editorial department after the editorial department has discovered there is no exit interview.

At 22:03 on New Year’s Eve, she was announcing a blog push for the next day. While other people counted down to a new year, Harshita was scheduling another draft. Her fireworks have suggested edits.

On 26/05/25 she opened with “guys lol ik this might be one more blog but” (26/05/25). Then: “pls iska bhi bata do I am so done telling 10000 ptrs” (26/05/25).

That first sentence is the sound a subscription makes when you thought you’d cancelled it.

The second is even better. She is done giving feedback, so she has brought the next feedback task to the group. Harshita doesn’t stop the production line; she rotates in a new shift of disappointed readers.

Her chats have the tonal range of a polite email with a stapler concealed in the attachment. Soft little acknowledgements, then a request to examine the premise, constraints and actual purpose of the entire thing. You think you’ve received a friendly acknowledgement. You have received reviewer two in a cardigan.

And on 02/08/25 she asked “Aisa agar main mugshot pictures maangu tumlog ka” (02/08/25). At last, a visual brief that accurately described the board. After enough revisions, every team photo becomes an evidence lineup.

12. Kairav Sheth: objections accepted from outside the service area

Kairav’s first contribution was a poll asking who would pay for the treats. The two options were enthusiastic participation and an admission of being too stingy. A beautifully neutral ballot from the Department of Pay Up.

By the next morning he was correcting the bill and signing it “From the desk of Kursi” (07/10/24). The board hadn’t completed a full day and the furniture had a finance office.

On 06/07/25 he described his writing with “Lekin drama nahi hota” (06/07/25) and “Na imagination hota hai” (06/07/25). Admirably explicit product specifications. Kairav promises neither entertainment nor escape. The paragraph will be load-bearing.

His definitive contribution came on 11/10/26, when he questioned the group’s enthusiasm for Ironhill. Garv asked whether he was even in Bengaluru. Kairav replied “Nahi” (11/10/26). Then he added “There are much better places around” (11/10/26).

Of course there are. Particularly around wherever Kairav isn’t attending.

He had gone 67 days between messages and returned to dispute a restaurant in another city. That is commitment to the opposition benches. Most people require presence to inconvenience a dinner; Kairav has implemented remote access.

He’s the man who declines the invitation but keeps edit access to the itinerary.

13. Sunny Gogoi: promoted directly from announcement to resignation

Sunny wanted the LinkedIn update on the first night. By 25/12/24 he wrote “Time to resign” (25/12/24). By 08/01/25 he was reporting “Was about to resign before term began” (08/01/25).

An entire career arc completed in the pre-joining paperwork. Sunny had achieved senior-management disillusionment without waiting for the inconvenience of formally starting.

The early title interest and early resignation interest are perfectly consistent. He wanted the achievement unlocked, not the unskippable cutscene afterwards.

During the 14/06/25 interviews he complained about wanting the session finished, then supplied the devastating scale of his endurance: “And I’ve been here 10 mins” (14/06/25). Ten minutes. The microwave was still warming the work experience.

Yet he remains on hand for procedural clarification, corrections and commentary. Sunny threatens to resign like a browser threatens to close while keeping thirty tabs open. There is always one more thing somebody is wrong about.

By September 2026 he was asking how to obtain his transcript and get into the alumni account. The LinkedIn launch had ended in an access-support queue. He began wanting proof that he belonged to the institution and finished wanting proof that he had successfully left it.

The college, correctly identifying a recurring customer, had locked the portal.

14. Shambhavi Paygude: the camera is off for quality-control reasons

Shambhavi’s first appearance in the group’s history is an identity correction: Tanush removed the wrong Shambhavi and added her. MadarBoard began by needing a second attempt at hiring Shambhavi. She has spent the subsequent chat demonstrating that one is already plenty.

In the merch debate she wanted the full domain name printed properly. Then her own name became a production issue. On 05/01/25 she asked “yaar 16 letters hai hoga fit? bohot chhota karna padega” (05/01/25).

Months of design governance, brought to its knees by the discovery that names have length. The board was debating institutional identity; Shambhavi was trying to fit hers between the seams.

Her other lasting contribution is laughter with an operational consequence. On 08/06/25 she wrote “abhi hasi band hui thi cam on karne waali ti” (08/06/25). She had almost restored visual service when somebody made the interview ridiculous again.

Across the export, 201 of her messages consist entirely of repetitions of 🤣. Not mixed text. Just that emoji, in varying quantities. A reaction button wasn’t enough; Shambhavi needed laughter to arrive as new correspondence.

She is the studio audience who also sits on the appointment panel. Somewhere a candidate is trying to demonstrate executive presence while one of the executives is waiting for her face to become professionally usable.

15. Srija Puvvada: reverse hide-and-seek

Srija sent 552 messages across 61 dates. In this group, that practically constitutes an oral tradition.

Her absence is not the whole joke. It’s the precision with which she appears to request context from a civilisation that has spent the interval manufacturing more context than anyone could possibly want.

On 12/06/25, mid-interview evening, she asked “hi whats happening what all q’s were asked?” (12/06/25). On 20/06/25: “wait what happened???????????” (20/06/25).

Srija visits the group like a property owner inspecting a flat she foolishly rented to twelve theatre students. Every visit reveals another improvised set, a new internal currency and somebody claiming the queue has feelings.

But don’t mistake low message volume for elevated detachment. On 15/06/25, she proposed “let s wager a cavin cold coffee” (15/06/25). The paneer-puff exchange had barely opened and she was already introducing another asset class. Arrives late, asks for the rules, immediately places a bet. Excellent use of a day pass.

Her last message in the export, on 12/11/25, is “Where are you guys” (12/11/25).

Then 350 days pass before the export ends, without another message from her.

An exquisite reversal. She asked where everyone else was, then became the location problem. The group finally received a question it could ponder at its preferred speed.

16. Meta AI: artificial intelligence, organic humiliation

Meta AI produced 135 messages. It deserves a board position solely for enduring a selection process conducted by people who already had an opinion about whether software could be rude to them.

On 08/11/24, Aastik asked it for speaker-session questions. Garv immediately asked it to tell Aastik to be quiet. Meta responded “Main chup rehne ki koshish karunga!” (08/11/24).

It offered to silence itself.

Even the machine saw an opportunity to withdraw and took it. Asked to manage Aastik, it chose personal disappearance. That is the most convincing evidence of reasoning in the whole export.

Meanwhile, the humans had a speaker they wanted to welcome, questions they wanted somebody else to ask, and an AI that had misunderstood who should stop talking. A magnificent chain of delegation with no final recipient.

Then the board developed Eshidis: a human pretending to be software while actual software was failing to interpret the humans. You built the world’s first computing chapter where the artificial intelligence was too agreeable and the database had personal boundaries.

Meta AI’s problem wasn’t hallucination. It was being trained to offer further assistance in a room where further assistance is how you get assigned another task.

17. The collective masterpieces: excellence, briefly misdirected

Your best moments are the ones where the coordination suddenly works for a purpose no sensible organisation would recognise.

The favourite-board interviews acquired a scoreboard, wagers, live commentary and repeated demands to ask the popularity question. You were selecting successors while also checking whether the candidates sufficiently appreciated the outgoing cast. It was a job interview held inside the judges’ employee-satisfaction survey.

The paneer puffs gave the proceedings stakes. Eshidis gave them a technological platform. Shambhavi supplied the audience. Manan got told to update the scoreboard. Aastik requested rankings and then challenged the emotional terms and conditions. A whole entertainment format, developed inside an activity that was supposed to test somebody else’s professionalism.

On 13/06/25, Yash claimed “eshidis in its prime could guarantee one million concurrent message requests” (13/06/25). This was a queue for a handful of people deciding who should ask the next question. You had invented an enterprise benchmark for raising your hand.

And the nostalgia! Eshidis already had a golden age. You could mourn the decline of infrastructure while still arguing over whether it existed. Most clubs struggle to build a legacy. You gave a running joke a collapse narrative within days.

The late-night conversations are equally impressive. On 02/07/25 at 03:30, amid the entertainment spiral, Manan announced “I lost the plot” (02/07/25)—about Marvel, specifically. Five minutes later, Supratim was investigating WhatsApp’s state management.

You could not follow a film franchise, but you were prepared to audit the architecture carrying your complaints about it.

That is MadarBoard at full power: thirteen people briefly capable of becoming a single organism, provided the shared objective is something utterly unnecessary.

18. Two years later: the institution has graduated; the dinner has not

June 2025 produced 31,946 messages. September 2026 produced 34.

The first figure suggests a country negotiating its constitution. The second suggests the caretaker checking whether anybody still uses the building. Neither tells the whole story, because even at reduced throughput you retained the ability to make a dinner require a small control room.

The October 2026 Bengaluru meet is your entire tenure miniaturised. Someone proposes meeting. Someone asks what the plan is. A poll appears. Kairav objects from elsewhere. Yash discusses discounts. People announce different forms of lateness. Booking details become a technical incident. Four days later, the split is still being chased.

The plot had not advanced. You had merely changed the city in which the plot was stuck.

Back in October 2024, Eshita wanted the first bill. In October 2026, Yash wanted the latest split. Between those two demands, you built projects, ran events, interviewed people, debated clothing and produced enough commentary to make every simple task feel like a director’s cut.

And the last visible textual prompt from Aastik, on 28/10/26, is “wanna try this for this group” (28/10/26). Whatever the omitted media contained, the familiar machinery was already turning: two years later, Aastik is still introducing another thing the group could try.

Of course he is. The suggestion box survived graduation. It now has a forwarding address.
